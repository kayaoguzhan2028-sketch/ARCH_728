The Ground Ankara Clay

Lost Streams

Beneath the dense, concrete topography of modern Ankara lies an active, invisible ecosystem. Often perceived as a dry, static steppe, the city was historically shaped by a vibrant hydrological network. Driven by rapid urbanization, these streams were buried under asphalt, transforming natural ecological corridors into subterranean infrastructural channels. However, the interaction between these trapped waters and the region's dynamic geological foundation reveals that the landscape is not a passive canvas, but an active agent in the urban environment.

1. The Concealed Hydrological Network

The urbanization policies of the mid-20th century prioritized rigid infrastructure over ecological preservation. In an effort to control floods and modernize the city, Ankara's primary watercourses were enclosed within concrete culverts. The lively valleys of Kavaklıdere and Bentderesi became heavily paved streets, while İncesu was diverted beneath urban parks and railway lines. Yet, these waters never vanished. They continue to flow in the dark, acting as the city's hidden hydrological memory, occasionally reclaiming their presence through urban flooding when the rigid infrastructure fails to accommodate natural surges.

3.1. Hatip Stream (Bentderesi)

Originating from the northeast (Kayaş-Mamak) and cutting through the steep cliffs beneath Ankara Castle, Hatip Stream was historically one of the most powerful waterways in the city. It derived its local name, Bentderesi, from historical dams (bents) built since the Roman era to regulate its flow.

Past & Usage: From antiquity until the mid-20th century, it was the social and recreational epicenter of Ankara. Its clean waters drew citizens for weekend picnics, swimming, and domestic use. The surrounding valley was lush with vineyards and orchards.

Closure Process: The rapid migration influx of the 1950s led to dense, unplanned gecekondus (slum settlements) along the valley slopes. Lacking basic infrastructure, these settlements dumped all domestic waste directly into the stream, triggering severe pollution, foul odors, and cholera outbreaks. The turning point was the catastrophic 1957 Ankara Flood. The massive loss of life and property prompted the municipality to completely enclose the stream within concrete culverts rather than restoring it.

Current Status: Today, the bustling Bentderesi Street in Ulus is an asphalt shell built directly over the riverbed. The stream flows through dark underground tunnels, heavily mixed with sewage, before meeting the Çubuk Stream.

3.2. İncesu Stream

Flowing from the slopes of Elmadağ and traversing the İmrahor Valley, the İncesu Stream historically intersected the city from south to north, playing a vital role in local sustenance.

Past & Usage: İncesu was crucial for the city's food supply, as its fertile alluvial basin hosted the famous 'İncesu Bostanları' (vegetable gardens). During the Early Republican era, it functioned as a major green lung and a natural landscape feature where citizens could interact with water.

Closure Process: The culverting began in the 1940s and continued progressively into the 1970s. As the urban core expanded southward toward Kızılay and Yenişehir, sections of the stream were channeled underground to mitigate flood risks and clear land for prominent new boulevards (such as Atatürk Boulevard).

Current Status: İncesu Street between Kolej and Seyranbağları traces the stream's exact natural path. It flows beneath Kurtuluş Park and Abdi İpekçi Park, which explains why these areas survived as green corridors. Recurring urban floods in Sıhhiye and Kurtuluş during intense downpours demonstrate the stream's persistent attempt to reclaim its natural topographic bed.

3.3. Kavaklıdere Stream

Descending from the Çankaya ridges, Kavaklıdere shaped the southern residential axis of the city. Though its volumetric discharge was lower than Hatip Stream, its ecological and climatic impact was profound.

Past & Usage: Named after the towering poplar (kavak) trees that lined its banks, this valley was a pristine summer retreat for Ankara residents in the late 19th and early 20th centuries. The clean air and scenic beauty of the valley were the primary reasons why early foreign embassies chose to establish their compounds in this district.

Closure Process: The rapid upscaling and construction of luxury apartment blocks in Çankaya from the 1960s onward vastly inflated land values. To maximize real estate development, the valley was leveled, and the stream was forced into narrow channels before being entirely entombed beneath roads.

Current Status: The valley path originates near Seymenler Park, moves down to Kuğulu Park, and runs along Tunalı Hilmi Street and İran Street. The iconic pond at Kuğulu Park is a direct, stylized remnant of the original natural pool formed by Kavaklıdere at that junction.

3.4. Capillary Streams: Hoşdere, Ayrancı, Bülbülderesi, and Cevizlidere

These smaller, localized waterways drained the southern hillsides of Çankaya, Dikmen, and Esat, feeding into the larger urban hydrological network.

Past & Usage: These streams sustained Ankara's renowned historical viticulture culture (such as the Ayrancı and Esat vineyards). They regulated soil moisture and acted as micro-climate cooling vents across the steppe terrain.

Closure Process: The massive cooperative housing and apartment boom of the 1970s and 1980s led to these narrow valleys being filled with construction debris. Topographies were artificialized, and the natural channels were directly hooked into the municipal sewage grid.

Current Status: Today, they correspond to highly dense residential quarters and major avenues: Hoşdere Street, Bülbülderesi Street, and the Cevizlidere Neighborhood. Because they flow directly beneath building foundations, structural degradation, chronic basement moisture, and foundation cracks due to the shifting underlying Ankara clay are frequently documented along these lines.

4. Conclusion

Ankara's entombed streams and restless clay expose the ultimate limitations of rigid, anthropocentric urban planning. The recurring floods and structural tensions are not mere engineering failures; they represent the active resistance of a landscape asserting its natural morphology. Recognizing this hidden environment is a prerequisite for a sustainable architectural and urban future—one that designs in permanent dialogue with the fluid and dynamic forces beneath the concrete crust.
