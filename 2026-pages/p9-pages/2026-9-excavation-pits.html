Ankara Clay The Ground Speaks

Excavation Dynamics

The urban expansion of Ankara has fundamentally altered its geological and topographical structure. The movement of soil—driven by rapid urban transformation, construction booms, and infrastructural projects—has created new spatial typologies: massive excavation dumping grounds and abandoned foundation pits. This report details the historical context, current status, and ecological implications of these excavation sites across the city.

1. The Rise of Excavation Mountains (Dumping Grounds)

As the city center expanded and densified, millions of cubic meters of soil were excavated and exiled to the urban peripheries. This massive displacement created new, artificial topographies.

Mamak and İncek Axes: These peripheral regions became the primary recipients of the city's construction waste. The accumulation of soil formed massive, amorphous "excavation mountains" that crushed the natural topography.

Çayyolu Region: Another major focal point for construction and excavation, where rapid urban transformation significantly altered the local ecological balance.

2. Abandoned Foundation Pits: The Urban Voids

The counterpart to the excavation mountains are the deep voids left behind in the urban core. Economic fluctuations, contractor bankruptcies, and protracted legal battles have caused numerous large-scale projects to halt abruptly, leaving deep scars in the urban fabric.

Susuz Neighborhood and West Courthouse Environs: Areas plagued by halted projects (e.g., Portaş or Seçim Group cases) where deep foundation pits remain abandoned, posing significant physical hazards.

Çukurambar and Söğütözü Axis: This axis hosts massive, water-filled foundation pits resulting from suspended high-rent projects.

Ghost Structures and Voids: The Togo Towers on the Eskişehir Road axis stand as sealed, functionless voids due to zoning plan cancellations. Similarly, the Iron Cage (Demir Kafes) and former EGO Hangars left massive undefined spaces in the city center.

Geological Interaction: The Formation of Artificial Ponds

The abandonment of these pits triggers a fascinating, albeit dangerous, geological reaction. When construction ceases and water pumps are turned off, groundwater and rainwater rapidly fill the deep excavations. Because the characteristic Ankara clay is highly impermeable, the water cannot drain downward. Consequently, these pits transform into deep, dangerous artificial ponds and micro-ecosystems, occasionally leading to tragic drowning incidents.

3. Official Interventions and Waste Management

Historically, illegal dumping in valleys and vacant lands (such as the İmrahor Valley, Eymir surroundings, and İvedik Organized Industrial Zone) caused severe ecological destruction. To combat this, the Ankara Metropolitan Municipality implemented strict monitoring and management measures:

GPS Tracking Systems: Thousands of excavation trucks are now monitored centrally. Any deviation from designated routes, unauthorized dumping, or suspicious waiting periods triggers immediate intervention by municipal authorities.

Designated Dumping Sites: Official, controlled excavation dumping grounds have been established in peripheral nodes such as Bağlum, Pursaklar (Karacaören), Sincan (Polatlar), and Ballıkuyumcu.

Heavy Sanctions: Severe administrative fines are applied to unauthorized dumpers. In many cases, offenders are forced to clean up their own illegally dumped rubble as a deterrent.

4. Ecological Reclamation and The "New Nature"

Despite the immense human-induced destruction, these excavation sites eventually undergo an ecological reclamation process, evolving into the city's "new nature."

Spontaneous Flora: In the disturbed soils of Çayyolu, İncek, and Mamak, invasive and ruderal plant species—such as thistles (Carduus) and wild mustard (Sisymbrium)—are the first to colonize the area, aggressively reclaiming the terrain.

Biodiversity Hotspots: Recent academic studies on urban voids suggest that these areas, often coded by the system as "derelict" or "neglected," actually become some of the richest biodiversity hotspots in the city. They represent the soil's silent rebellion against the rigid concrete infrastructure.
