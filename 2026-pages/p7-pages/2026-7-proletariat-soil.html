Proletariat Soil

The proletarian, unlike the vagabond, is not left idle but put to work. The human no longer moves this soil to be rid of it; it moves it to build. Extracted, crushed, mixed, laid down. The metabolism that once held all matter within the same geography is gone, and in its place there is only extraction. This soil is no longer nature; it is produced. "(Social) space is a (social) product," Lefebvre writes, and this is the matter that product is made of: gravel stirred into petroleum for the asphalt, pressed flat beneath the tiling of a park. The only place it is still called nature is the surface laid out for the eye, and even there nothing is exchanged beneath it.

The worker who raises the building cannot live in it, and the soil that holds up the city will never inhabit it. It produces the place and is shut out of it. It carries everything, and owns nothing. And here is the strange thing: the building is also soil. The tower is the same matter laid beneath the asphalt, crushed and bound and put to work, so the civilized and the proletarian are not two soils but one, shown from two sides: the named front the human calls civilization, and the working back that is never seen. The barbarian still moved. The vagabond still sat as nature, however idle. This soil does neither: it cannot migrate, and it can no longer die. What rises on top of it, the human calls civilized.

Dönem
