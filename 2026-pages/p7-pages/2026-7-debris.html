Debris

When the human forgets that it will die, it controls its surroundings. Right here, in this moment, it begins to see its own laws as higher than nature. The feeling runs so deep that it orders and fixes everything around it, as if all of it would stay the same forever. For the human, what stays inside, where its laws apply, is enough. Whatever does otherwise is thrown outside the borders, outside the polis.

So everything carried away is the barbarian: the soil that threatens the laws the human built while feeling immortal, the soil that will not comply. The nomad's free mobility appears here as political punishment. This too is not nature; it is a human decision. The soil is reworked in places far from sight, and in the meantime it is forgotten. Some barbarians may be taken back into the order, if they have passed through the right regulation. Others cannot. The human has reworked the soil. But at what cost?

Debris is not a subject or an object, but rather a record of a moment. It is in a constant state of transformation. Unlike "rubble," which implies reusable or identifiable building pieces, debris refers to matter dispersed by violence or disaster. It captures not only physical remnants but also the broader spatial transformation caused by destruction.
