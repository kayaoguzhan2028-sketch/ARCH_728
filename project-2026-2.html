What the Ground Carries?

Stratigraphic Memory and the Politics of Urban Soil Metabolism
on Hıdırlık Hill, Ankara

Nisa Gezer, Selen İlhan, Serda Buket Erol, Ülkü Karakaş

Hıdırlık Hill has been continuously transformed through extraction, settlement, demolition, and reconstruction. Things have been taken from the hill, things have been added to it. Its current form is the result of these successive interventions, each one covering or erasing what came before. This project traces those layers in the (urban) soil. We position ourselves as soil detectives, reading the hill not through its current surface but through the seams where one ground ends and another begins. Our central claim is that the "greening" narrative renders the hill's transformation invisible at precisely the moment when it is most consequential. Biological life is destroyed, decades of inhabitation are sealed beneath concrete, and the hill's original stratigraphy is quietly erased.

What the ground carries is not merely material, it is political.

How can we uncover the conditions of erasure on Hıdırlık Tepesi, and what does the ground still carry from what has been removed?

Where does the soil of Hıdırlık go when the hill is "greened", and what does the new ground carry that the old ground no longer can?

How does urban soil metabolism function across centuries of transformation, and what does its interruption make visible?

Our project combines archival research, spatial analysis, and three-dimensional modelling to trace the successive transformations of Hıdırlık Tepesi across several centuries. The archival layer draws on historical maps, cadastral records, aerial photographs, planning documents, geological surveys, and press coverage to reconstruct what occupied the hill at each critical moment of change. These materials are not treated as background context but as primary evidence. Each document is read for what it reveals about the ground itself, what was built on it, what was extracted from it, what was buried beneath it, and who was removed from it? The spatial layer translates this evidence into a single three-dimensional model, in which all temporal states of the hill coexist simultaneously. The model is not a reconstruction of any single historical moment but an instrument for reading the relationships between moments, collisions, erasures, and continuities that would remain invisible in a conventional chronological account.
