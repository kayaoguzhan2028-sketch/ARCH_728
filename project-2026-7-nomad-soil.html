Nomad Soil

The first form of soil. Not the product of any order, but of a movement that obeys the laws of nature. This soil is nomadic because its movement, too, comes from the same source. Under the wind it lets its sediment be carried; under the rain it sets into form; under the earthquake it breaks. All of its migration is natural. And so the plants, the animals, the microorganisms that live upon it and within it obey what the soil obeys.

The crucial distinction here is the figure of the human, who pulls itself out from among the animals. The human sets itself apart, and transforms that same soil, sows it and reaps it. As Donna Haraway writes: "Humankind is self-made in the most literal sense. Our bodies are the product of the tool-using adaptation which pre-dates the genus Homo. We actively determined our design through tools that mediate the human exchange with nature." Yet the tool does not lift the human out of nature. It is how the human enters the exchange, not how it escapes; the human works the soil and is worked by it in return. The human does not stand above what it works. Soil is an actor in the exchange between nature and the human. And it is here that the nomad shows itself: it is nomadic only because nothing yet holds it in place. No line has been drawn, no parcel has been cut. As the soil migrates with everything it carries, the living things upon it and within it remain bound to it.

The name of this exchange is metabolism, Stoffwechsel. What the human sows, it reaps and eats, or feeds to its animals. The waste returns, again, to the soil. In this exchange all matter remains within the same geography; the human does not interfere with the movement of the soil. This is the order of nature. The human grows old and dies, and is given back to the soil. So too its animals, so too its plants. And after a time the soil can no longer give the human enough. The season turns, the waterways shift. As the human ages, so the soil ages. When the human's relation to the soil falls into suspension, the human does not resent it, and migrates to another geography until the soil gathers itself again. The soil, in its own way, dies and is reborn in the meantime. New sediment arrives, the old drifts away. This exchange goes on without end, at times in excess, at times scarce, but always in measure. The human knows that it will die; the soil knows that it will migrate.
