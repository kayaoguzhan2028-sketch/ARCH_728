Urban Soil Metabolism · Spring 2026

Reflections on Landscape

“Urban Soil Metabolism” examines soil as a living, active urban medium — continuously produced, displaced, transformed, and regulated through intertwined human and non-human forces. Developed within the graduate course ARCH 728: Reflections on Landscape at the METU Department of Architecture, the research traces the multiscalar, temporal, and processual dimensions of landscape by bringing landscape knowledge into dialogue with architecture, urbanism, infrastructure, and ecology. Focusing on Ankara, it foregrounds soil’s agency, revealing the ground as an active site of planetary environmental crises and urban transformation.

Through this lens, soil is approached not as a stable substrate but as a medium whose presence, vitality, and movement are continuously negotiated within the city. The research unfolds around three interconnected concepts — visibility, (in)habitability, and transportability — each tracing a distinct dimension of soil’s urban agency.

Visibility

When does soil become visible — and when is it rendered invisible in the city?

Soil becomes visible at moments of disruption, failure, or intervention, and recedes from view through stabilization, coverage, and standardization. Excavation, contamination, and infrastructural breakdown bring soil to the surface of urban attention, while in everyday conditions it is aestheticized, sealed, or folded into the background of urban life. The projects trace the practices, representations, and thresholds through which soil enters — and exits — public awareness.

(In)habitability

When is soil alive, inert, or waste? And whose labor sustains it?

Across urban processes, soil shifts between conditions that enable or restrict its capacity to host life. What is treated as a living medium in one context may be rendered inert or disposable in another through changes in structure, compaction, contamination, and management. This distinction is not merely biological but political: it rests on a regime of care, sustained by often unseen labor, in which certain grounds are maintained as valuable landscape while others are cast as “matter out of place” to justify their neglect. The projects examine how social definitions of purity and waste shape the conditions under which soil becomes inhabitable — or is actively rendered otherwise.

Transportability

What does soil carry and transform as it moves?

As soil circulates through the city, it transports more than matter. It carries traces of previous sites, ecological relations, and social decisions. Transport alters its composition, meaning, and potential as materials are displaced, mixed, and reassembled. The projects follow soil as a carrier, reading transformation through movement rather than origin alone.
