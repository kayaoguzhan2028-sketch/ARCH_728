Displaced Volume: Soil Mobility and the Ecological Afterlives

This study emerges from a critical engagement with contemporary debates that reconceptualize urban soil not as a stable ground, but as a dynamic, relational, and metabolically active medium. Drawing on perspectives that frame soil as produced (Meulemans, 2020), stratified and politically charged (Clark, 2016), volumetrically governed (McNeill, 2020), metabolically circulating (Harris), and as a living infrastructure requiring care (Puig de la Bellacasa, 2014), this research focuses on the mobility of urban soil and its unintended consequences. Territorial differentiations and mobility of volumes of soil are in question at Bilkent area. In Ankara, particularly through the large-scale excavation processes associated with the construction of Bilkent City Hospital, significant volumes of soil have been displaced and redistributed across this area. This process includes not only the physical relocation, but also the transfer of embedded biological, chemical, and hydrological properties, which may result in diverse unintended species to spread.

Within this context, this study offers to examine the territorial grounds within Bilkent area in 3 contexts in terms of movement's consequences: sterilized, incomplete and refurbished. Hospital and its surroundings, the Beytepe Pond and the arboretum (Botanical Park's incomplete parts) are approached as a critical site where these dynamics become materially observable. In an area expected to exhibit steppe characteristics, the emergence of wetland vegetation — such as reeds, dead nettle and land snail — and within excavation-related grounds suggests that transported soils carry ecological potentials that become activated under new environmental conditions. Rather than a simple anomaly, this phenomenon can be read as the reappearance of ecological relations and histories embedded within the soil visible in a different spatial context.

Within this framework, this study adopts soil metabolism as the central conceptual framework through which the displaced soil of Bilkent are read and assessed within 3 contexts. Following Harris (2025), soil is understood not as an object but as a process, sustained through histories of circulation and transformation — what Harris terms soils in motion. This perspective allows the excavated volumes mobilized by the City Hospital construction to be approached not as inert fill or extra material, but as metabolically active ground carrying biological, chemical, and hydrological properties across the urban territory. This research departs from conventional Urban Metabolism frameworks, which, as Harris argues, tend to reduce soil to an inert stock quantified through input–output flows, thereby preventing its ecological specificity. Instead, this study foregrounds the life/non-life distinction that organizes the urban soil: the shifting attribution of liveliness through which soil is alternately rendered active or passive in response to regulatory thresholds (Harris, 2025). Reading the Hospital territory, Beytepe Pond and the unfinished arboretum through this lens, the continually emergent reeds, dead nettle, and land snails are interpreted as material evidence for the underground water streams and that soil's stability is fiction. The contexts gain importance with this reading due to the fact that even in the sterile concrete landscape of the hospital territory seemingly unfertile gains some qualities that enables instances of reeds, land snail, pollinator pair, dead needle, etc. around the territory such as the bed road due to the spread of same subjected excavated soil.

By engaging with how soil moves and behaves (Barrera-Bassols & Zinck, 2003, cited in Harris, 2025), the research positions soil not as a passive substrate to be stabilized, but as an active participant whose displacement reactivates ecological archives and reconfigures the metabolic relations between ground, water, and vegetation across the Bilkent area. Refurbished area of botanic recalls another perspective that distinct species that are not innate are intentionally implanted and grown to create a designed landscape which includes labels and descriptive elements to specify what is what. However, in incomplete context include scenarios with wilderness and random growth of innate landscape figures as well as carried elements such as random trees of pine, reeds among non-watery stream bed resulted in wilderness. Lastly the hospital area reflects on the qualities of a designed environment with sterile, structured and concrete elements. This area does not reflect transition of fertile environment, rather on contrary the soil that seem un-fertile becomes fertilized when it is spread around the region towards the bed of the road, arboretum and more.

Instead of treating such formations as exceptions, this study interprets them as indicators of a disrupted yet generative soil metabolism. The movement of soil through excavation practices produces new assemblages between ground, water, and vegetation, destabilizing conventional distinctions between natural and artificial environments. In this sense, the research is guided by the following questions: How does the mobility of urban soil reconfigure new ecological conditions and generate unexpected habitats? In the case of partial observation (Beytepe Pond, Bilkent City Hospital and arboretum) do the emergent plant formations prove the erasure of the steppe, or do they reveal the soil's capacity to retain and rearticulate its own ecological archive in a new location? Through these questions, the study aims to reposition soil not as a passive substrate to be stabilized, but as an active agent whose circulation reshapes urban ecologies, produces new forms of habitability, and simultaneously carries and reactivates traces of past environments.

Reference: Harris, L. (2025). Soils in motion: the metabolism of New York City's urban grounds. Landscape Research, 50(8), 1287–1302. https://doi.org/10.1080/01426397.2025.2526498

Methodology

This research adopts a multi-scalar and interpretive methodology to investigate the mobility of urban soil and its ecological consequences in Ankara. This is achieved through the examination of Bilkent area in 3 contexts in terms of movement's consequences: sterilized, incomplete and refurbished. Hospital and its surroundings, the Beytepe Pond and the arboretum (Botanical Park's incomplete parts) are approached as a critical site where these dynamics become materially observable. Rather than treating soil as a fixed and stable ground, the study approaches it as a dynamic, circulating, and metabolically active medium. To capture this condition, the methodology combines spatial mapping, field observation, territorial relationality, experimental orthographic projections, field observation, and critical material interpretation through plant species analysis.

At the core of the study is the tracing of soil movement generated through large-scale excavation practices, particularly those associated with the construction of Bilkent City Hospital. This is achieved through spatial mapping techniques and site analysis that identify the points of extraction, transportation, and deposition of soil. Temporal analysis, supported by satellite imagery and planning documents, is used to understand how these movements unfold over time and how they correlate with changes in land use and ecological conditions. Field observations conducted in and around Beytepe Pond provide a situated understanding of these processes. Particular attention is given to soil composition, surface conditions, moisture patterns, and the emergence of vegetation such as reeds in areas where such ecologies would not typically occur. These observations are not treated as isolated data points, but as indicators of broader material and ecological transformations. The botanical park intro building and half-finished entry are targeted to observe the uncompleted construction and vegetation processes resulted in wilderness, remaining in the center of a designed area. This part reflects on the ever-changing status of the existing ecological particles and bare refurbishment with material flows, ecological continuity, hydrological relations and open space.

The methodology also incorporates a material reading of soil as a stratified and relational entity. By examining the physical characteristics and concealing water features within the ground, the study interprets soil as both a carrier of past conditions and a medium through which new ecological assemblages emerge. This approach is complemented by critical reading of planning documents, archival research, aerial photographs and site visits which reveal how soil is differently framed across institutional, ecological discourse, and everyday discourses. Importantly, this research does not seek to establish linear causality between soil movement and ecological change. Instead, it employs an interpretive and relational approach that foregrounds the connections between excavation, hydrological systems, and emergent vegetation. Through this framework, soil is understood not only as material, but also as archive — retaining, transporting, and rearticulating ecological traces across urban space.
